ILIA x CENIA


Mapping, Governance, and Technological Sovereignty in Latin America and the Caribbean
The conference led by Antonia Moreno exposed in an analytical manner the state of the art, the strategic deployment, and the mapping of the Artificial Intelligence (AI) ecosystem in Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC). The core of the argument demonstrated that the region is not a mere passive consumer of global technology, but an emerging pole of development with a dense network of researchers, startups, and academic consortia that articulate Machine Learning and Deep Learning solutions that are computationally viable and adapted to the problems of the Global South.


Diagnosis of the Ecosystem and Mapping of Actors Through the inter-institutional work of ILIA and CENIA, the urgency of structuring a data infrastructure and a topological map of key stakeholders (academia, private sector, regulators, and open-source communities) was underlined. The identification of these nodes allows to:

  1. Optimize technological transfer: Reduce the gap between theoretical research and industrial deployment.
  2. Detect asymmetries: Locate gaps in investment, advanced computing infrastructure (such as GPU clusters), and talent drain.
  3. Foster synergies:Design evidence-based public policies and regional innovation clusters.

Data Ethics, Representation, and Bias Mitigation
An analytical pillar of the talk centered on Responsible AI and Algorithmic Governance. Current AI finds itself at a critical point where software centralization perpetuates systemic biases. The speakers emphasized that:

  1. Foundational models and Large Language Models (LLMs) are usually trained with predominantly Anglo-Saxon or Eurocentric datasets, which generates a representativeness bias when applied in LAC.
  2. It is imperative to develop local and curated datasets that reflect the linguistic, socioeconomic, and cultural diversity of the region, guaranteeing an equitable, inclusive AI with a low rate of algorithmic bias.

Technological Sovereignty and Strategic Impact The closing of the event positioned the development of AI not only as a competitive economic advantage, but as a geopolitical necessity and an act of technological sovereignty. Relying exclusively on APIs and proprietary models from foreign corporations generates a critical infrastructural vulnerability.
Key Conclusion: Digital sovereignty in Latin America demands the design and implementation of AI architectures conceived from the regional idiosyncrasy. Only through the control of the complete data cycle —from its collection and preprocessing to the fine-tuning of the models— will the region be able to guarantee its economic autonomy, mitigate technological dependence, and lead the resolution of its own structural challenges.

  3. Technological Geopolitics: The concept of "sovereignty" was elevated to the current technical-economic framework, where AI defines which nations will lead the productivity of tomorrow.
